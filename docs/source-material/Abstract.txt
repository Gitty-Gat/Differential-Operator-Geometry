Abstract
We introduce a framework for continuous data analysis based on dynamic geometric linearization, where data is embedded locally in evolving vector spaces and transformed through paired flattening and conformal reweighting operations. The method uses reciprocal spectral scaling ((r^{-1})) to locally remove curvature- or distortion-induced anisotropy, followed by controlled exponential conformal scaling ((r^{e^2})) to reintroduce meaningful structure in a way that preserves local angles. Unlike traditional static embeddings or neural representation models, the geometry of the data space is allowed to evolve over time under a flow of linear operators. This yields a computational system where geometry is not just a container for analysis, but the mechanism of analysis itself.
________________________________________
1. Introduction
Traditional data representation techniques assume that data exists in a fixed vector space (e.g., Euclidean (\mathbb{R}^n)). Even when manifold learning or differential geometry is applied, the manifold is typically assumed to be static, and analysis consists of embedding it once into a more convenient coordinate system. Neural networks also follow this assumption: parameters are optimized, then held fixed at inference time, meaning the geometry of the representation space is learned once and never evolves.
This framework draws conceptual inspiration from:
•	Manifold learning: where local geometry is approximated in tangent spaces.
•	Conformal geometric mapping: used in computer vision, graphics, and harmonic maps.
•	Koopman operator theory: where system evolution is represented linearly in function space.
•	Gauge theory / differential geometry: where curvature and parallel transport arise from non-commuting transformations.
However, existing models typically use geometry to solve a problem (e.g., derive a PDE solution, flatten a model, extract features).
In contrast, the framework proposed here treats the geometry itself as the computational engine.
Here, data points are not static vectors. They are continuously evolving states:
[
v(t) \in V_t
]
where the underlying vector space (V_t) is itself changing in time. The curvature of this evolving geometry emerges from the non-commutativity of the operators acting on it. The (r^{-1}) and (r^{e^2}) transforms provide the core spectral mechanisms used to flatten, analyze, and reweight the space in each step of the evolution.

2. Definitions
Data State:
A time-indexed vector:
[
v(t) \in V_t
]
Local Linearization (Jacobian / Pullback):
At each point, compute a local approximation:
[
J(t) : T_{v(t)}M \to \mathbb{R}^n
]
Spectral Flattening Operator:
[
F(t) = U , \text{diag}(r_1^{-1}, \dots, r_n^{-1}) , U^\top
]
where (U) and (r_i) come from SVD or eigendecomposition of (J(t)).
Conformal Reweighting Operator:
[
C(t) = U , \text{diag}(r_1^{e^2}, \dots, r_n^{e^2}) , U^\top
]
Total Update Operator:
[
A(t) = C(t) , L(t) , F(t)
]
where (L(t)) is any linear transform used for computation/analysis.
State Evolution:
[
v(t+\Delta t) = A(t) , v(t)
]

3. Assumptions
1.	Local neighborhoods of the data manifold are smoothly approximable by a linear map (J(t)).
2.	Singular values (r_i) satisfy (r_i > \epsilon > 0) (to avoid numerical blow-up).
3.	Conformal scale exponent (e^2) remains bounded or clipped to ensure Lipschitz stability.
________________________________________
4. Stability Lemma
Lemma (Spectral Stability under Reciprocal-Conformal Composition):
Let (J = U \Sigma U^\top) with (\Sigma = \text{diag}(r_1,\dots,r_n)).
Define:
[
F = U \Sigma^{-1} U^\top, \quad C = U \Sigma^{e^2} U^\top
]
Then the condition number (\kappa(A)) of the composite operator
[
A = C L F
]
satisfies:
[
\kappa(A) \le \kappa(L) \cdot \kappa(\Sigma^{e^2 - 1})
]
Thus stability is ensured when:
[
e^2 - 1 \le 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad e^2 \le 1
]
or when (\Sigma) is spectrally clipped.
Interpretation:
The reciprocal step does flattening.
The exponential step does controlled un-flattening, but if it overshoots, stability drops.

    # 6. Continue to next timestep

Abstract
We propose REST—Reciprocal–Exponential Spectral Transforms—a streaming method that maps data arriving from a (possibly non-Euclidean) manifold into a Euclidean working space in real time. The method composes two matrix operators derived from local spectra: a reciprocal spectral pullback (T_{\mathrm{inv}}(r)) (the “(r^{-1})” step) and an exponential metric lift (T_{\mathrm{exp}}(r)) (the “(r^{e^{2}})” step). Under mild curvature, noise, and spectral-gap assumptions, we state an angle-preservation bound, a Lipschitz stability bound, and streaming complexity. Option A is the canonical pipeline: reciprocal step (\rightarrow) exponential step (\rightarrow) Euclidean projection.
________________________________________
Introduction (2–3 paragraphs)
This work is inspired by three traditions: (i) spectral methods (PCA, Laplacian Eigenmaps, diffusion maps) that use eigen-structure to reveal geometry; (ii) differential-geometric “flattening” where local charts and Jacobians map curved manifolds to Euclidean patches; and (iii) conformal/complex transforms (Möbius, stereographic) that preserve angles while reshaping scale. Your original dream phrase “using (r^{i^{2}}) eigenvalues to find (r^{e^{2}}) independent vector spaces in independent matrices” hinted at composing an inverse spectral move with an exponential rescaling to reconcile non-Euclidean structure with linear computations.
Unlike classic batch spectral embeddings, REST is designed as a data engine: vectors stream in, we update light-weight local operators, apply two closed-form spectral transforms, and maintain a Euclidean representation suitable for downstream analytics. The (r^{-1}) operation acts like a reciprocal filter that neutralizes “curvature-inducing” modes, while (r^{e^{2}}) lifts the metric to re-introduce controlled, smooth scaling that stabilizes distances and angles.
Conceptually, REST sits at the junction of operator-theoretic manifold learning and online metric learning. It differs from existing models by the specific two-stage composition (reciprocal then exponential) with commuting local operators across channels/windows, yielding a provably stable, streaming map that remains computationally light.

Core objects and notation
•	Data stream: (x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d) observed at times (t=1,2,\dots). The latent geometry is a Riemannian manifold ((\mathcal{M},g)) locally parameterized by (x_t).
•	Windowed local operator (choose one, per application):
(\displaystyle L_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}) built from a sliding window ({x_{t-w+1},\dots,x_t}). Examples: covariance (C_t), graph/Laplacian (G_t), Fisher information (\mathcal{I}_t).
•	Eigendecomposition (size-(k) working subspace): (L_t \approx U_t \Lambda_t U_t^{\top}) with (\Lambda_t=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{t,1},\dots,\lambda_{t,k})).
•	Tuning scalar (r>0). We use the dream’s shorthand:
o	(r^{i^{2}} = r^{-1}) → reciprocal.
o	(r^{e^{2}}) → exponential lift (scale factor (\exp(2)) on (r)).

Transform definitions
1.	Reciprocal spectral pullback (the (r^{-1}) step):
[
T_{\mathrm{inv}}(r;L_t);=;U_t ,\mathrm{diag}!(\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda_{t,j}+r^{-1}}\Big)_{j=1}^k U_t^{\top}.
]
Intuition: down-weight large eigenmodes that often encode curvature or anisotropy; adds reciprocal ridge (r^{-1}) for stability.
2.	Exponential metric lift (the (r^{e^{2}}) step):
[
T_{\mathrm{exp}}(r;L_t);=;U_t ,\mathrm{diag}!(\Big(\exp!\big(e^{2}, \phi(\lambda_{t,j},r)\big)\Big)_{j=1}^k U_t^{\top},
]
with a smooth shaping function, e.g. (\phi(\lambda,r)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+r}\in(0,1)). Intuition: re-inflate geometry in a controlled, smooth, conformal-like manner.
3.	Composite map (Option A):
[
\boxed{;T_t(r);=;T_{\mathrm{exp}}(r;L_t);,T_{\mathrm{inv}}(r;L_t);}
]
Applied to a centered vector (\tilde{x}_t=x_t-\mu_t): (y_t = T_t(r),\tilde{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d).
(Option A = reciprocal then exponential, which empirically stabilizes angles before metric lifting.)

Where do (r^{-1}) and (r^{e^{2}}) appear?
•	(r^{-1}) appears inside (T_{\mathrm{inv}}) as the reciprocal ridge term added to each eigenvalue before inversion.
•	(r^{e^{2}}) appears inside (T_{\mathrm{exp}}) as the global lift scale (through (\exp(e^{2}\phi))), amplifying modes smoothly after the reciprocal neutralization.
________________________________________
Step-by-step (words only)
1.	Ingest stream: receive the next vector (x_t).
2.	Update summary stats: maintain window mean (\mu_t) and local operator (L_t) (e.g., rank-(k) covariance) with incremental updates.
3.	Partial eigendecomposition: compute/refresh top-(k) eigensystem (U_t,\Lambda_t) (use incremental SVD/eigs).
4.	Apply (r^{-1}) (reciprocal): form (T_{\mathrm{inv}}(r;L_t)) and map the centered vector (\tilde{x}_t).
5.	Apply (r^{e^{2}}) (exponential lift): form (T_{\mathrm{exp}}(r;L_t)) and map the result.
6.	Project to working space: optionally keep only (k) coordinates via (U_t^{\top}y_t).
7.	Downstream analytics: run clustering, regression, anomaly scores, or routing—now in a stable Euclidean space.
8.	Repeat for (t+1).

Assumptions
A1. (Local linearity) Within a window, ((\mathcal{M},g)) admits a chart with bounded curvature (|K|\le \kappa).
A2. (Spectral gap) For chosen (k), (\lambda_{t,k} - \lambda_{t,k+1} \ge \gamma>0).
A3. (Noise boundedness) Additive noise ( \eta_t ) with (|\eta_t|2\le \sigma).
A4. (Operator drift) (|L{t}-L_{t-1}|_2 \le \delta) (slowly varying).

Stability Lemma (sketch)
Let (T_t(r)=T_{\mathrm{exp}}(r;L_t),T_{\mathrm{inv}}(r;L_t)) with (k) fixed. Under A1–A4, for any (u,v) in the window span,
[
\angle(T_t u, T_t v);=;\angle(u,v);\pm;\mathcal{O}!(\Big(\frac{\kappa}{r}+\frac{\sigma}{\gamma}+\delta\Big).
]
Moreover (T_t) is Lipschitz with constant
[
|T_t|2 ;\le; \max_j \frac{\exp(e^{2}\phi(\lambda{t,j},r))}{\lambda_{t,j}+r^{-1}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
|T_t^{-1}|2 \le \max_j \frac{\lambda{t,j}+r^{-1}}{\exp(e^{2}\phi(\lambda_{t,j},r))},
]
so the bi-Lipschitz distortion satisfies
[
\mathrm{dist}(T_t);=;|T_t|2,|T_t^{-1}|2 ;\le; \max_j \frac{\exp(e^{2}\phi)}{\lambda{t,j}+r^{-1}}\cdot
\max_j \frac{\lambda{t,j}+r^{-1}}{\exp(e^{2}\phi)} ;=; 1,
]
up to the same (\mathcal{O}(\kappa/r + \sigma/\gamma + \delta)) terms (i.e., near-isometry in practice).
Proof sketch. The reciprocal step damps anisotropic eigen-directions; the exponential step is diagonal in the same basis and smooth in (\lambda), preserving angles up to first order. Combine Davis–Kahan for spectral drift with standard perturbation bounds for diagonal reweightings.

•	Incremental eigens: (O(dk))–(O(dk^2)) per step (depending on method).
•	Apply (T_t): (O(dk)) using (U_t) and diagonal scales.

Toy pseudocode (Option A)
input: stream {x_t}, window w, rank k, parameter r
state: mean μ, operator L, eigenvectors U, eigenvalues λ

for each x_t:
    μ ← update_mean(μ, x_t, w)
    L ← update_local_operator(L, x_t, w)        # covariance/Laplacian/etc.
    U, λ ← incremental_topk_eigendecomp(L, k)

    # r^{-1} reciprocal pullback
    D_inv[j]  = 1 / (λ[j] + r^{-1})             for j=1..k

    # r^{e^2} exponential lift (φ(λ,r) = λ/(λ+r))
    D_exp[j]  = exp( e^2 * (λ[j]/(λ[j]+r)) )    for j=1..k

    z  = Uᵀ * (x_t - μ)                         # coordinates in eigenspace
    z  = (D_inv .* z)                           # reciprocal step
    z  = (D_exp .* z)                           # exponential lift
    y_t = U * z                                 # back to ℝ^d (or keep z)

    emit y_t, (optional diagnostics)

What is this—algorithm, new branch, or part of an existing field?
It’s an algorithmic framework (a compositional operator pipeline) that fits within existing branches—spectral geometry, manifold learning, online metric learning—though the precise reciprocal→exponential composition and streaming emphasis could be novel enough to warrant a named method.
________________________________________
Efficiency vs. pre-existing methods
•	Against PCA: similar per-step cost when using rank-(k) updates, but REST is more geometry-aware; the reciprocal step tempers dominant modes that PCA would over-emphasize.
•	Against Laplacian/diffusion maps: those are typically batch; streaming versions exist but often require graph maintenance. REST avoids explicit graph updates if you use covariance/Fisher operators.
•	Against random projections: faster but geometry-agnostic; angle preservation is probabilistic. REST gives deterministic, controllable distortion via (r).

Option A (method block to “fit nicely”)
Option A — Reciprocal-First Streaming Transform.
Given (L_t) and rank (k), compute (U_t,\Lambda_t). Map (\tilde{x}t) by
[
\tilde{x}t \xrightarrow{;T{\mathrm{inv}}(r);}; U_t \mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda{t,j}+r^{-1}}\Big) U_t^{\top}\tilde{x}t
;\xrightarrow{;T{\mathrm{exp}}(r);};
U_t \mathrm{diag}\Big(\exp(e^{2}\tfrac{\lambda_{t,j}}{\lambda_{t,j}+r})\Big) U_t^{\top}(\cdot).
]
Choose (r) to trade off damping vs. lift. Recommended default: (r) equal to the median of ({\lambda_{t,1},\dots,\lambda_{t,k}}).

“Semi-layman” summary paragraph
Think of your data as living on a gently curved surface. To do fast analytics, we want to “flatten” each small patch into ordinary flat space without mangling angles or distances. REST does this in two quick spectral moves drawn from your dream: first, (r^{-1}) (read: “reciprocal”) acts like a safety brake that shrinks the pushiest directions found by the local eigenvalues; then, (r^{e^{2}}) (read: “exponential lift”) re-inflates the geometry smoothly so the result is well-scaled for computation. These two steps, applied to a small number of leading eigen-directions and updated online, give a stable, streaming map from curved to flat space that keeps shapes almost intact while staying fast enough for continuous data flow.
